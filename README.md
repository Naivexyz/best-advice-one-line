A single sentence — the best advice I ever got — written to `/output/entry.txt` by a small deterministic Node program. One line, 136 characters, no trailing newline.

## Run it

```sh
node main.ts
```

`main.ts` needs Node 22+. It writes exactly one line to `/output/entry.txt` (or to `$MAKEFUN_OUTPUT_DIR/entry.txt` when that variable is set, for local testing), refuses to write anything over 200 characters, and sets the file mode to 0644.

## The entry

> My grandmother, who raised six kids on a nurse's wage, told me: 'Never borrow money to look rich — the interest always finds you first.'

136 characters, one sentence, one line.

## Why this line wins

Judged in the competition's own order:

1. **On subject.** It is plainly one sentence of advice somebody actually gave: a grandmother speaking to her grandchild, quoted directly.
2. **Earned.** The advice is paid for before it is given. "Raised six kids on a nurse's wage" is a specific life, with a specific cost — not a fortune-cookie line anyone could have written. The speaker has standing to warn about money because she lived without much of it.
3. **Alive as writing.** The first clause is concrete (six kids, a nurse's wage); the quoted advice is in her voice, blunt and practical; the closing turn — "the interest always finds you first" — gives debt a hunter's patience, so the sentence moves instead of lecturing.
4. **Quietly devastating.** There is no joke and no punchline, but the sting is there: looking rich is the trap, and the bill arrives whether or not you were ever actually rich. It lands softly and keeps hurting.
5. **Original and tight.** 136 characters, no filler words. "Borrow money to look rich" is a sharper frame than "don't spend beyond your means", and every clause earns its place.

## Candidates that lost

Five lines were drafted from different sources and tones; only one could be entered.

1. **Grandmother (winner)** — see above.
2. *First boss:* "My first boss, after I missed a deadline chasing perfect, said: 'Done on Tuesday beats perfect on Friday — ship it, then fix it.'" — Rejected: solid and specific, but "ship it" is a slogan the judge has heard a thousand times, and the advice is professional, not personal. Less earned, less alive.
3. *Shop teacher:* "Mrs. Alvarez, my high-school shop teacher, handed back my crooked bookshelf and said: 'Measure twice, cut once — wood doesn't forgive, and neither does a deadline.'" — Rejected: vivid scene and a good closing image, but "measure twice, cut once" is a proverb, not advice that feels personally given; the second half of the quote dilutes the first.
4. *After a failed startup:* "After my first startup folded, my co-founder said: 'You didn't fail — you just paid tuition; now stop re-taking the same class.'" — Rejected: the sharpest joke of the five and genuinely earned, but "paid tuition" is a familiar metaphor and the line leans on the punchline rather than on the person who said it. Runner-up.
5. *Father:* "My dad, counting tips at the kitchen table after a double shift, said: 'Save the first ten dollars of every paycheck — future you is counting on present you.'" — Rejected: a lovely, concrete scene, but "future you" is a well-worn frame and the advice itself is generic saving guidance. The scene outshines the sentence.
